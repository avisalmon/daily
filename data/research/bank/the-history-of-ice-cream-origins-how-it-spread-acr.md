# The History of Ice Cream

**Introduction**  
We all scream for ice cream – a beloved frozen treat enjoyed worldwide today. But this dessert has a surprisingly long history, stretching back **over 2,000 years** to ancient civilizations in Asia and the Middle East ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=surprisingly%20long%20history%2C%20stretching%20back,every%20civilization%20along%20the%20way)). Over the centuries, many cultures devised **frozen desserts** made from ice or snow mixed with sweet flavors – simple beginnings for the creamy treats we now know ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=surprisingly%20long%20history%2C%20stretching%20back,every%20civilization%20along%20the%20way)). As it traveled across the globe, each region added its own ingredients and techniques, transforming ice cream from an *elite luxury* into a mass-market delight available to all.

## Ancient Origins  
The story of ice cream begins in antiquity. An early key was discovering **how to obtain and store ice** in warm climates. The ancient Persians appear to have been pioneers in this regard: by around **550 BC** they had developed **yakhchāls**, large domed *“ice-pit”* structures with deep underground chambers insulated by thick earthen walls ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=was%20originally%20developed%20in%20Persia,High%20domes)). These beehive-shaped ice houses were built in the desert and ingeniously designed to harvest winter ice and keep it frozen through the summer for use in food preservation and chilled treats ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=was%20originally%20developed%20in%20Persia,High%20domes)). Stored ice was mixed with fruit, honey, or rosewater to create early sorbets and **frozen sweets** – for example, the Persians made *faloodeh*, a dish of thin noodles in a semi-frozen syrup of sugar and **rose water**, as a refreshing dessert ([theconversation.com](https://theconversation.com/inside-scoop-the-2-500-year-history-of-ice-cream-270156#:~:text=metres%20%E2%80%93%20about%203%20million,1368)). The idea of using ice and snow to cool sweet liquids or fruit also arose independently elsewhere. **Ancient Chinese** records from around the 2nd century mention blending snow with milk and rice for a frozen delicacy ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=putting%20their%20own%20spin%20on,By%20the%2019th)). In the **Roman Empire**, Emperor *Nero* (1st century AD) would dispatch runners to the mountains for fresh snow, which was then flavored with honey and fruit juices to create chilled desserts ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=credited%20with%20its%20discovery,Over%20a%20thousand%20years)).

 *An ancient Persian **yakhchāl** (ice house) in Yazd, Iran, built with a domed mud-brick structure. These ingenious refrigerators, first built around 400 BCE, could freeze water and store ice in the desert climate, enabling early Persians to cool foods and create frozen desserts like faloodeh ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakhch%C4%81l#:~:text=Workers%20clearing%20ice%20from%20ice,Humayun%20%28r)) ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=was%20originally%20developed%20in%20Persia,High%20domes)).*

## Cross-Cultural Spread  
From these ancient origins, the concept of icy sweets spread across the Old World. After the 7th-century **Arab conquest of Persia**, knowledge of ice storage and dessert-making traveled throughout the Middle East ([theconversation.com](https://theconversation.com/inside-scoop-the-2-500-year-history-of-ice-cream-270156#:~:text=to%20make%20frozen%20desserts%20such,1368)).  Arabic cooks pioneered **sharbats** – sweet chilled drinks made with fruit juice and sugar – and experimented with adding milk, sugar, and flavorings to ice, laying the groundwork for true ice cream and sherbet** ([theconversation.com](https://theconversation.com/inside-scoop-the-2-500-year-history-of-ice-cream-270156#:~:text=to%20make%20frozen%20desserts%20such,1368))**.  In the medieval Islamic world, these treats were often perfumed with ingredients like rosewater and topped with nuts or dried fruits ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=mention%20frozen%20blends%20of%20milk,By%20the%2019th)). Meanwhile, the *Mughal* rulers of India adopted Persian ice techniques in the 16th century, having ice blocks transported from the Himalayas and preserved in **baraf khanas** (ice pits) to make **kulfi**, a dense frozen dairy dessert flavoured with saffron and pistachios ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakhch%C4%81l#:~:text=using%20saltpetre.%20The%20ab,7%5D%20Although%20many%20have)). In East Asia, Japan developed its own tradition of shaved ice: by the 10th century, the imperial court savored **kakigōri**, finely shaved ice topped with sweet syrups, as recorded by Lady Sei Shōnagon during the Heian period ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream#:~:text=includes%20recipes%20for%20sweet%20desserts,written%20by%20Sei%20Sh%C5%8Dnagon%2C%20who)). And in imperial **China**, a frozen milk-based confection called **“shī shuǐ”** or *sushan* (“crispy ice”) emerged during the **Tang Dynasty** (618–907 CE); it was made from tempered goat’s milk mixture frozen in decorative molds, yielding a dessert that was “between a liquid and a solid” and melted in the mouth ([theconversation.com](https://theconversation.com/inside-scoop-the-2-500-year-history-of-ice-cream-270156#:~:text=booza%20and%20bastani%2C%20in%20Persia,the%20science%20of%20freezing%20Freezing)). Each culture contributed something unique – new flavors, ingredients, or techniques – to the evolving art of ice cream.

Europe came to know ice desserts through Middle Eastern and Asian influences. It is often claimed that **Marco Polo** brought a recipe for a sherbet-like frozen treat from **China** to **Italy** in the late 13th century, and that this may have inspired Italian cooks to create their own gelatos ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=Claudius%20Caesar%20%28A.D.%2054,was%20introduced%20to%20similar%20frozen)). While the **Marco Polo story** is likely more legend than fact, frozen desserts certainly reached **Renaissance Europe** via travel and trade. Italian chefs excelled at making *sorbetto* (fruit ices) and began incorporating dairy to create creamier ices. One early account notes that *“cream ice”* was being served at the court of England’s King Charles I in the 1600s ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=evolved%20into%20ice%20cream%20sometime,introduced%20a%20recipe%20blending%20milk)). In **France**, Italian-born **Catherine de’ Medici** is credited with introducing frozen delicacies like sorbets when she married into French royalty in 1533 ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=Italians.%20,introduced%20a%20recipe%20blending%20milk)). By the late 17th century, what we would recognize as **true ice cream** – made with milk or cream, sugar, and flavorings – appeared in Europe. Cookbooks by *Antonio Latini* in Italy (1694) and *Nicolas Audiger* in France (1692) documented recipes for milk-based **sorbets and ice creams**, including techniques for churning and smoothing the mixture to create a creamy texture ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=cream%20recipes%20emerge%20at%20almost,and%20candied%20fruits%2C%20and%20is)). At the same time, entrepreneurial vendors began selling ices to the public. In 1686, Sicilian chef **Francesco Procopio** opened Paris’s first café to serve **iced desserts**, Cafe Procope, offering a blend of milk, cream, butter, and eggs frozen into what we would now call ice cream ([www.britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ice-cream#:~:text=recipes%20and%20techniques%20for%20making,Washington%2C%20Thomas%20Jefferson%2C%20and%20Dolley)). Once a novelty for nobility, ice cream was gradually becoming an attainable treat for those outside royal circles ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=Damascus%2C%20and%20Cairo%2C%20often%20flavored,ice%20cream%20affordable%20and%20available)).

By the 1700s and 1800s, **ice cream spread to the Americas** as a delight of the elite. Wealthy colonists and leaders in the United States indulged in ice cream on special occasions – a 1744 letter from a colonial governor’s guest described ice cream being served at a dinner in Maryland ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=America%20The%20first%20official%20account,of%20Mount%20Vernon%20taken%20after)). The first known advertisement for ice cream in America appeared in 1777, when a New York City confectioner offered it “almost every day” during the summer ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=letter%20written%20in%201744%20by,spent%20approximately%20%24200%20for%20ice)). President **George Washington** was so fond of ice cream that he reportedly spent around \$200 on it during the summer of 1790 (a huge sum at the time) ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=May%2012%2C%201777%2C%20when%20confectioner,President%20Thomas)). **Thomas Jefferson** even penned his own vanilla ice cream recipe (an 18-step process), and **Dolley Madison** served an impressive strawberry ice cream at the White House in 1813 ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news-views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=merchant%20show%20that%20President%20George,Madison%27s%20second%20inaugural%20banquet%20at)) ([www.britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ice-cream#:~:text=other%20ice%20makers%20soon%20opened,Louis)). Still, making ice cream was labor-intensive and expensive, requiring ice harvested from nature and painstaking hand-churning, so it remained a *luxury item* enjoyed mainly by the wealthy ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_M._Johnson#:~:text=American%20inventor%20best%20known%20for,Early%20life)).

## From Luxury to Mass-Market: Technological Advances  
In the 17th century, scientists in Europe made breakthroughs that would eventually allow ice cream to be produced more easily. A **major discovery** was the use of *saltpeter* (potassium nitrate) or plain salt mixed with ice to drastically lower the freezing temperature, which made it possible to freeze sweet mixtures on demand ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=alchemy%E2%80%94was%20first%20published%20by%20Giambattista,easier%20to%20freeze%20liquids%2C%20because)) ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=alchemy%E2%80%94was%20first%20published%20by%20Giambattista,revealed%20a%20similar%20reaction%20occurs)). In 1558, Italian scientist **Giambattista della Porta** published *Magia Naturalis*, describing how adding saltpeter to snow could “congeal [a vial] by degrees” and freeze its contents quickly ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=Discovering%20the%20science%20of%20freezing,easier%20to%20freeze%20liquids%2C%20because)). By the late 1600s, recipes for ices recommended packing the ingredients in a bucket surrounded by salt and ice while stirring, to produce a smoother, creamy result rather than an icy slush ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=cream%20recipes%20emerge%20at%20almost,and%20candied%20fruits%2C%20and%20is)). Another key ingredient was the increasing availability of *sugar*. Earlier frozen sweets had been sweetened with honey or fruit syrups (since refined sugar was scarce), but by the 17th–18th centuries European colonies in the Caribbean were exporting cheap cane sugar, allowing ice creams to become **sweeter and creamier** ([amazingfoodanddrink.com](https://amazingfoodanddrink.com/history-of-ice-cream-the-origins-of-frozen-desserts-across-cultures/#:~:text=people%20relied%20on%20honey%20to,Once%20sugar)) ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=potassium%20nitrate%20dissolved%20in%20water,and%20Italy%20in%20the%201690s)). With salt-and-ice brine techniques and ample sugar, making ice cream became easier and less costly, setting the stage for it to move from royal banquet tables to the wider public.

The **industrial era** of the 19th and 20th centuries brought rapid innovation that turned ice cream into a mass-market product. In the **1840s**, American inventor **Nancy Johnson** created the first hand-cranked ice cream **freezer** – a portable device with a tub, a lid, and a rotating paddle (dasher) – which she patented in 1843 ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_M._Johnson#:~:text=American%20inventor%20best%20known%20for,Early%20life)). Her invention mechanized the laborious stirring process by means of a crank and a dasher inside a chilled container, drastically reducing the time and effort needed to make ice cream at home or in small shops ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_M._Johnson#:~:text=primarily%20to%20wealthy%20households.%20,to%20make%20ice%20cream%20while)). This breakthrough “**transformed ice cream production from a labor-intensive process into a more efficient and accessible practice, helping shift the dessert from a luxury item to a widely consumed product**” ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_M._Johnson#:~:text=American%20inventor%20best%20known%20for,Early%20life)). Just a few years later, in 1851, milk dealer **Jacob Fussell** established the first **commercial ice cream factory** in Baltimore, using surplus cream from dairies to churn out ice cream in quantity and ship it by railroad to other cities ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Fussell#:~:text=operated%20a%20dairy%20business%20for,Seven%20Valleys%20resident%2C%20Daniel%20Henry)). Fussell’s venture was so successful that he expanded to multiple cities, earning him the nickname “**Father of the Ice Cream Industry**” in America ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Fussell#:~:text=Washington%2C%20D,Seven%20Valleys)). By the late 19th century, ice cream manufacturing had adopted steam power and early mechanical refrigeration, allowing production in **factories** year-round rather than only in winter when natural ice was available.  

Into the 20th century, new technologies and products fueled explosive growth of ice cream’s popularity. The development of electrical refrigeration for homes and businesses meant ice cream could be made, stored, and transported more easily. In **1926**, inventor **Clarence Vogt** introduced the first continuous-process ice cream **freezer**, an automated machine that could nonstop pump out ice cream on an industrial scale ([www.history.com](https://www.history.com/articles/ice-cream-boom-1920s-prohibition#:~:text=Countertop%20freezers%20allowed%20busy%20soda,started%20with%20the%20onset%20of)). This continuous freezer allowed ingredients to be added on one end and soft ice cream to emerge from the other, vastly increasing production volume and slashing labor costs – leading to the “true mass marketing” of ice cream by the 1920s ([www.history.com](https://www.history.com/articles/ice-cream-boom-1920s-prohibition#:~:text=Countertop%20freezers%20allowed%20busy%20soda,started%20with%20the%20onset%20of)). At the same time, companies devised *novel treats* to make ice cream more portable and appealing to consumers. The 1920s saw the launch of chocolate-covered ice cream bars (the original **Eskimo Pie**), ice pops like the **Popsicle**, and ice cream cups (e.g. **Dixie Cups**), all of which could be sold in stores and at events in **single-serving** packages ([www.history.com](https://www.history.com/articles/ice-cream-boom-1920s-prohibition#:~:text=included%20the%20expansion%20of%20soda,grew%20into%20behemoths%20and%20soda)). The invention of the **ice cream cone** – popularized at the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair – also gave people a fun, edible way to enjoy ice cream on the go ([www.britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ice-cream#:~:text=States%2C%20ice%20cream%20was%20served,Louis)). These innovations, along with the expansion of refrigeration-equipped soda fountains and shops, firmly entrenched ice cream as a popular consumer product by the mid-20th century ([www.history.com](https://www.history.com/articles/ice-cream-boom-1920s-prohibition#:~:text=Hulton%20Archive%2FGetty%20Images%20A%20man,Vogt%E2%80%99s)).

 *A vintage **Good Humor** ice cream truck (circa 1920s). Early ice cream vendors like Harry Burt outfitted trucks with freezers and bells to sell ice cream bars in neighborhoods ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Humor#:~:text=Harry%20Jr,1922%2C%20Burt%20applied%20for%20patents)), bringing frozen treats directly to the masses and creating the first mobile ice cream parlors.*

During **Prohibition** in the United States (1920–1933), ice cream’s popularity got an extra boost. With alcohol banned, Americans turned to soda fountains and sweet treats for socializing; breweries and bars even converted into ice cream parlors to survive the dry years ([www.history.com](https://www.history.com/articles/ice-cream-boom-1920s-prohibition#:~:text=Busch%20and%20Yuengling%20turned%2C%20in,growth%20of%20the%20ice%20cream)). The U.S. military helped, too: ice cream was served as a morale booster for troops during World War II, with the Navy commissioning floating ice cream parlors to treat sailors in the Pacific ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/news%E2%80%93views/media-kits/ice-cream/the-history-of-ice-cream#:~:text=The%20History%20of%20Ice%20Cream,product%20rationing%20was%20lifted%2C%20America)). By war’s end, ice cream had become firmly cemented as an **American staple**, no longer just a warm-weather luxury but a year-round indulgence. In 1939, the first **soft-serve** ice cream was invented (by dairy pioneers like **Dairy Queen** and others), using a machine to dispense smoother soft ice cream straight from the freezer without hardening ([www.britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ice-cream#:~:text=is%20ripened%20for%20several%20hours,was%20invented%20in%201939%3B%20it)). In the postwar era, refrigeration technology became ubiquitous in homes, allowing millions of families to stock ice cream in their own freezers. Mass advertising and the franchise model (from soda fountains to fast-food drive-ins) further propelled ice cream into everyday life.

## Ice Cream Today  
**Modern ice cream** is a global industry and an integral part of food culture around the world. What was once a rare delight for emperors and elites is now produced on an enormous scale – global ice cream sales were estimated around **\$80–125 billion** annually in the mid-2020s and still growing as new markets emerge ([www.idfa.org](https://www.idfa.org/ice-cream-sales-trends#:~:text=Fortune%20Business%20Insights%20estimates%20the,year%2C%20or%20about%204%20gallons)). Hundreds of flavors and varieties exist today – from classic **vanilla and chocolate** to regional specialties – and there are countless related frozen desserts (gelato, sorbet, sherbet, frozen yogurt, etc.). Many countries have developed their own ice cream novelties and traditions, from Italian *gelato* and Turkish *dondurma* (stretchy, taffy-like ice cream) to Japanese *mochi ice cream* and Filipino *halo-halo*. Ice cream is enjoyed in cups, cones, shakes, sundaes, and more, and it continues to evolve with new technologies (like instant liquid-nitrogen freezing) and trends (such as plant-based or dairy-free ice creams).

Despite its modern **year-round availability**, ice cream remains especially linked with summertime pleasure and nostalgia. Interestingly, the world’s highest ice cream consumption per capita today is found not in the hottest climates but in New Zealand, followed closely by the United States and Australia ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=promotion%20where%20prices%20changed%20as,by%20The%20Conversation%20Who%27s%20behind)). Even nations known for cold weather – Norway, Sweden, Canada, and Denmark – rank among the top consumers of ice cream ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-01-scoop-year-history-ice-cream.html#:~:text=promotion%20where%20prices%20changed%20as,by%20The%20Conversation%20Who%27s%20behind)). Whether enjoyed on a hot day to cool off or in winter as a comfort food, ice cream has become a *universal treat*. From its origins in ancient ice houses and royal courts to modern factories and neighborhood ice cream trucks, the journey of ice cream reflects human ingenuity and our enduring love of sweet, frozen delights.
