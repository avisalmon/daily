## Introduction  
Trust in institutions, information, and even fellow humans has been under strain for decades – long before today’s surge of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. Many indicators show that **public trust in media and political institutions has been declining in numerous countries for years** ([news.gallup.com](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651977/americans-trust-media-remains-trend-low.aspx#:~:text=much%E2%80%9D%20confidence,of%20Democrats%2C%2027)) ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/crisis-of-political-trust-global-trends-in-institutional-trust-from-1958-to-2019/7EF4EDA709F27C691380CFC1BCECF6B8#:~:text=differences%20between%20countries%20and%20regions%2C,support%20crisis%20of%20democracy%20trust)). The “AI era,” marked notably by the late-2022 debut of powerful generative AI (e.g. ChatGPT), has raised urgent questions about whether **this new technology is accelerating or altering these trust trends, or if it’s merely coinciding with pre-existing declines**. To disentangle **(a) long-term trends** from **(b) recent accelerations** and **(c) direct causal effects of AI**, we examine five key facets of trust in the context of AI’s rise: (1) *interpersonal trust between people*, (2) *trust in AI systems themselves*, (3) *trust in media and information*, (4) *trust in politicians and institutions*, and (5) *trust within workplaces (including employer–employee trust and AI-mediated hiring/monitoring)*. We prioritize recent academic research (2023–2026) and longitudinal surveys (e.g. Edelman Trust Barometer, OECD Trust Survey, Pew, Eurobarometer, World Values Survey, Reuters Institute Digital News Report, Gallup, and the KPMG-University of Melbourne “Trust in AI” study) to gauge how trust is changing and whether AI is genuinely a cause.

## Interpersonal Trust Between People  
**Long-term data indicate that generalized interpersonal trust has been stagnant or declining in many societies, a trend predating modern AI**. For example, in the United States, the **General Social Survey** found the share of adults saying “most people can be trusted” fell from **46% in 1972 to 34% in 2018**, and a **2023–24 Pew survey found the figure remained at **34%** ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/2025/05/08/americans-trust-in-one-another/#:~:text=Webster%2C%20Reem%20Nadeem%20and%20Janakee,People%20learn%20to%20trust)). This suggests a decades-long decline in social trust (especially from the 1970s to 1990s), with levels plateauing at historically low levels in recent years. Globally, **an Ipsos poll (Feb–Mar 2022, 22,534 adults in 30 countries)** found that **on average only 30% of adults agreed that “most people can be trusted.”** However, interpersonal trust varies widely by country, from **around 56% in China and India to under 15% in Brazil, Malaysia, and Turkey** ([www.ipsos.com](https://www.ipsos.com/en/interpersonal-trust-across-the-world#:~:text=most%20people%20can%20be%20trusted,say%20so)). Notably, **Nordic countries tend to report high social trust (e.g. Denmark ~74%, Norway ~72%)** while many low-income or politically unstable countries report very low trust ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/self-reported-trust-attitudes#:~:text=value%20from%20the%20closest%20year,Maldives%2021)) ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/self-reported-trust-attitudes#:~:text=Latvia%2022,Bolivia)). Overall, **the evidence points to a pre-existing long-term trend (category a)** of eroding social cohesion and trust in many (though not all) societies ([www.worldvaluessurvey.org](https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/WVSNewsShow.jsp?ID=774#:~:text=dimensions%E2%80%94interpersonal%20and%20institutional%E2%80%94within%20different%20socio,paper%20concludes%20by%20emphasizing%20the)). There is **no clear sign thus far that the advent of generative AI has significantly altered interpersonal trust**. Factors like economic inequality, polarization, and social changes are often cited as drivers of low social trust ([www.worldvaluessurvey.org](https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/WVSNewsShow.jsp?ID=774#:~:text=dimensions%E2%80%94interpersonal%20and%20institutional%E2%80%94within%20different%20socio,paper%20concludes%20by%20emphasizing%20the)), and these were in motion long before AI’s recent rise. In summary, **interpersonal trust remains relatively low in numerous countries, continuing a long-running trend** (with some national exceptions) that **cannot be attributed in any direct way to generative AI**.

## Trust in AI Systems Themselves  
With AI technologies becoming more prevalent, **trust in AI **as a class of systems** has become an important new metric – and recent survey evidence suggests a mixed and *worsening* picture since the emergence of advanced generative AI (category b)**. A **global study led by the University of Melbourne and KPMG (surveying 48,000 people across 47 countries in late 2024)** found that **while 66% of people use AI regularly, only 46% are willing to trust AI systems** ([mbs.edu](https://mbs.edu/news/Global-study-reveals-trust-of-AI-remains-a-critical-challenge#:~:text=although%2066,become%20less%20trusting%20and%20more)). This indicates that **well under half of the global public currently trusts AI**. Moreover, comparisons to earlier data show a **decline in trust coinciding with the rise of generative AI in 2022**: in the 17 countries surveyed before ChatGPT’s release, the share of people willing to trust AI fell **from about 63% in 2022 to 56% in 2023** ([www.unleash.ai](https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/news/66-of-individuals-may-be-using-ai-but-79-are-wary-of-possible-risks-finds-kpmg#:~:text=was%20keen%20to%20dig%20into,clear%20role%20to%20play%20when)), accompanied by a jump in concern about AI’s risks (the proportion “worried about AI” rose from ~49% to 62% over that period) ([www.unleash.ai](https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/news/66-of-individuals-may-be-using-ai-but-79-are-wary-of-possible-risks-finds-kpmg#:~:text=was%20keen%20to%20dig%20into,clear%20role%20to%20play%20when)). While these shifts are **correlational**, they suggest **a recent acceleration of public skepticism (category b)** around AI as its capabilities – and potential for misuse – have become more apparent.  

**Trust in AI varies dramatically by region and demographic**. The KPMG-Melbourne survey revealed a **“clear split” between emerging and advanced economies: roughly *three in five people (60%)* in emerging economies say they trust AI, compared to only *two in five (40%)* in advanced economies** ([www.channelnewsasia.com](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/emerging-economies-lead-way-ai-trust-survey-shows-5095876#:~:text=material%20from%20Mediacorp%20and%20Mediacorp%E2%80%99s,in%20these%20countries%20and%20the)). In other words, **people in countries like China, India, Brazil, or Kenya express far greater trust in AI on average than those in Western Europe or North America** ([www.channelnewsasia.com](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/emerging-economies-lead-way-ai-trust-survey-shows-5095876#:~:text=material%20from%20Mediacorp%20and%20Mediacorp%E2%80%99s,in%20these%20countries%20and%20the)). Researchers attribute this gap to factors like **perceived benefits of AI** – which tend to be more immediate or significant in developing economies – **as well as differing cultural attitudes towards technology** ([www.channelnewsasia.com](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/emerging-economies-lead-way-ai-trust-survey-shows-5095876#:~:text=material%20from%20Mediacorp%20and%20Mediacorp%E2%80%99s,in%20these%20countries%20and%20the)). Meanwhile, early evidence on **public–expert divides** indicates that **AI *experts* often have nuanced views: they see AI’s promise but are also acutely aware of its limitations and risks**. For instance, a May 2023 **Bentley University–Gallup survey** of 5,458 U.S. adults found that while majorities believe **AI equals or exceeds human performance in some tasks** (e.g. 68% say AI performs *as well as or better than humans* at personalizing online content), they are **much more skeptical about AI in high-stakes domains** – roughly **two-thirds of Americans say AI is *worse* than humans at deciding whom to hire, driving cars, or providing medical advice** ([news.gallup.com](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/510635/three-four-americans-believe-reduce-jobs.aspx#:~:text=bring%20in%20some%20areas,to%20use%20AI%20responsibly%2C%20a)). This shows the public currently **trusts AI more in narrow, low-risk applications but prefers human decision-makers for critical decisions** (a stance that predates generative AI and likely falls under category a). However, **even experts note that trust in AI is complex and context-dependent** – building “trustworthy AI” will require improving transparency, governance, and public understanding ([aiadvisoryboards.wordpress.com](https://aiadvisoryboards.wordpress.com/2025/04/30/trust-attitudes-and-use-of-artificial-intelligence-a-global-study-2025-kpmg/#:~:text=match%20at%20L72%20,attitudes%20compared%20to%20advanced%20economies)) ([kpmg.com](https://kpmg.com/au/en/insights/artificial-intelligence-ai/trust-in-ai-global-insights-2025.html#:~:text=The%20KPMG%20and%20University%20of,AI%20technologies%2C%20careful%20management%20and)). In short, **trust in AI systems themselves does not appear to be rising with AI’s popularity – if anything, global trust in AI has declined since late 2022 (category b)**, and **public confidence in AI’s use remains much lower for sensitive tasks, especially among publics in advanced economies**.

## Trust in Media and Information  
**Public trust in news and information sources has been on a long-term downward trend in many democracies (category a)**. The **Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2023** – based on a massive survey of 93,000 online news consumers across 46 countries – found that **only 40% of people on average said they trust “most news, most of the time,” down 2 percentage points from the previous year** ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2023/dnr-executive-summary#:~:text=their%20political%20views,Finland)). This modest decline in 2022–23 effectively **reversed the temporary rise in media trust seen during the COVID-19 pandemic**, resuming a prior decades-long slide ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2023/dnr-executive-summary#:~:text=their%20political%20views,Finland)). (For example, U.S. media trust spiked in 2020 at the pandemic’s outset, then fell again.) In the **United States**, trust in mainstream media is near historic lows: as of a late 2024 **Gallup poll (Sept 2024, ~1,500 U.S. adults)**, **only 31% of Americans have a “great deal or fair amount” of confidence in the mass media**, essentially tied with the record low of 32% first recorded in 2016 ([news.gallup.com](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651977/americans-trust-media-remains-trend-low.aspx#:~:text=WASHINGTON%2C%20D.C.%20,1970s%2C%20trust%20ranged%20from%2068)). By contrast, in the early 1970s roughly 70% of Americans trusted the media – illustrating a **precipitous long-run decline**. Notably, **for the third year in a row, more Americans say they have *no trust at all* in the media (36%) than say they have at least a fair amount of trust (31%)** ([news.gallup.com](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651977/americans-trust-media-remains-trend-low.aspx#:~:text=WASHINGTON%2C%20D.C.%20,1970s%2C%20trust%20ranged%20from%2068)). This U.S. erosion of media trust started long *before* recent AI developments (driven largely by political polarisation and misinformation on social media), so **there’s no clear evidence that generative AI caused a new drop**. The recent 2-point dip in global news trust from 2022–23 ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2023/dnr-executive-summary#:~:text=their%20political%20views,Finland)) is **consistent with pre-existing trends (category a)** rather than a sudden *acceleration* due solely to AI.  

That said, **the AI era has intensified concerns about information integrity, and many commentators fear a “liar’s dividend” – i.e. that the mere existence of AI-generated fake content will further erode trust in *authentic* media** ([www.brennancenter.org](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/deepfakes-elections-and-shrinking-liars-dividend#:~:text=various%20potential%20consequences%20arising%20from,about%20deepfakes%20poses%20a%20threat)) ([www.brennancenter.org](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/deepfakes-elections-and-shrinking-liars-dividend#:~:text=educated%20about%20the%20threats%20posed,Establishing%20norms%20against%20these%20lies)). Surveys show the public *is* highly aware and worried: in an August 2023 **YouGov poll of Americans**, **85% said they were “very” or “somewhat” concerned about the spread of misleading “deepfake” videos and audio** ([www.brennancenter.org](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/deepfakes-elections-and-shrinking-liars-dividend#:~:text=Goldstein%20Andrew%20Lohn%20Published%20January,awareness%20of%20the%20power%20of)). However, it remains **disputed how much this awareness has translated into additional loss of trust (categories b/c)**. **Academic research has begun testing these fears**. A 2024 study in *American Political Science Review* simulated scenarios of politicians invoking “fake news” or “deepfake” claims to dodge scandals – a prime example of the liar’s dividend tactic – and found that these **false claims helped politicians shore up support among their base, *but did *not** significantly reduce overall trust in media** ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073#:~:text=ineffective%20against%20video%20evidence%20and,Information%20American%20Political%20Science%20Review)) ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073#:~:text=or%20reduced%20trust%20in%20the,the%20measure%20of%20belief%20used)). In other words, the notion that generative AI will *automatically* devastate media trust is **not yet backed by empirical evidence (the hypothesis of a major causal effect (c) remains unproven)**. Still, experts caution that as AI-generated misinformation grows more sophisticated, it *could* worsen the ongoing trust decline in media (an example of category b, if it accelerates an existing trend) – a possibility that news organizations are actively trying to counter via heightened verification and transparency measures ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/rebuilding-trust-journalisms-role-ai-driven-world#:~:text=generative%20AI%20content%20must%20be,generated%20summaries%2C%20and)). 

## Trust in Politicians and Institutions  
**Trust in political institutions (governments, parliaments, elected officials, etc.) has long been falling in many countries** (again reflecting a *pre-AI trend – category a)*. The **OECD’s 2023 cross-national Trust Survey (nearly 60,000 people in 30 countries)** reports that **on average just 39% of citizens had “high or moderate” trust in their national government in 2023, down slightly from 43% in 2021** ([www.slideshare.net](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/oecd-survey-on-the-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results/270185229#:~:text=The%20OECD%20survey%20reveals%20a,handling%20of%20complex%20policy%20issues)). Some nations – notably many **Western democracies – have experienced *decades*-long declines in political trust**. In the U.S., for instance, **public trust in the federal government to “do the right thing” most of the time fell from ~73% in 1958 to around 20% in recent years** ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/#:~:text=right%20thing%20almost%20always%20or,By%201980%2C%20only)). By June 2023, **only 16% of Americans said they trust the government in Washington almost always or most of the time**, one of the lowest levels in seven decades of surveys ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/#:~:text=always%E2%80%9D%20%282,Public%20trust%20in%20government%20near)) ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/#:~:text=government%20in%20Washington%20to%20do,Center%2020%2020%204%2F11%2F2021%20Pew)). Similar drops in trust toward *representative* institutions have been documented globally. A 2025 study analyzing thousands of surveys since 1958 concluded that **“globally, trust in representative institutions has generally been declining in recent decades”** ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/crisis-of-political-trust-global-trends-in-institutional-trust-from-1958-to-2019/7EF4EDA709F27C691380CFC1BCECF6B8#:~:text=differences%20between%20countries%20and%20regions%2C,support%20crisis%20of%20democracy%20trust)). (Interestingly, the same study found trust in certain **“implementing” institutions – like militaries, courts, or civil services – has been stable or even rising** ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/crisis-of-political-trust-global-trends-in-institutional-trust-from-1958-to-2019/7EF4EDA709F27C691380CFC1BCECF6B8#:~:text=differences%20between%20countries%20and%20regions%2C,support%20crisis%20of%20democracy%20trust)), showing that not *all* institutional trust trends are uniformly negative.) Importantly, **these broad declines long predate modern AI** – driven by factors such as political polarization, corruption, and performance failures ([www.worldvaluessurvey.org](https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/WVSNewsShow.jsp?ID=774#:~:text=dimensions%E2%80%94interpersonal%20and%20institutional%E2%80%94within%20different%20socio,paper%20concludes%20by%20emphasizing%20the)) – and there is **no robust evidence that generative AI has yet caused any abrupt shift in political trust (no confirmed category c effect)**. 

**However, some experts worry that AI-enabled disinformation and “deepfakes” might further corrode trust in elections and institutions over time** ([www.brennancenter.org](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/deepfakes-elections-and-shrinking-liars-dividend#:~:text=or%20%E2%80%9Csomewhat%20concerned%E2%80%9D%20about%20the,content%20or%20video%20footage%20is)) ([www.brennancenter.org](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/deepfakes-elections-and-shrinking-liars-dividend#:~:text=avoid%20accountability%20will%20become%20more,that%20outcome%20need%20not%20upend)). Key upcoming events, like the 2024–2026 election cycles in various countries, have fueled *speculation* that AI-generated fake news or bogus videos could accelerate the erosion of trust in leaders and democratic processes (potentially a category b scenario if it occurs). *So far*, though, such fears remain largely hypothetical. In experimental conditions, exposure to political misinformation and even fake “deepfake” claims has **not consistently produced a large collapse in voters’ trust in the media or democracy** ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073#:~:text=ineffective%20against%20video%20evidence%20and,Information%20American%20Political%20Science%20Review)) ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073#:~:text=could%20further%20undermine%20trust%20in,analysis%20plans%2C%20and%20our%20research)). Yet the risk may grow if malign actors exploit AI to create confusion – for example, a convincing fake video of a politician could prompt some citizens to doubt real evidence or lose faith in the political process. **In short, trust in politicians and institutions was already low and falling in many places (category a), and while generative AI has raised new alarms, any definitive impact on these trends remains unproven and contested (no clear category c effect to date)**.

## Trust Within Workplaces (Employers, Employees, and AI in HR)  
Workplace trust operates on multiple levels – employees’ trust in their employers, employers’ trust in employees, and trust in new AI-mediated processes – and each has felt pressures in the digital/AI era. **Surveys suggest employees generally have *higher trust in their own employer* than in other institutions**. The **2023 Edelman “Trust at Work” report** found a “stunning” **23-point higher trust in “My Employer” compared to societal institutions overall among employees (23-point *above* the general trust index) ([www.edelman.com](https://www.edelman.com/trust/2023/trust-barometer/special-report-trust-at-work/my-employer-higher-expectations#:~:text=The%202023%20Edelman%20Trust%20Barometer,and%20ten%20points))**. This implies many workers continue to view their direct employers as comparatively trustworthy (often more so than governments or media), a pattern that has even *strengthened* slightly over the past few years ([www.edelman.com](https://www.edelman.com/trust/2023/trust-barometer/special-report-trust-at-work/my-employer-higher-expectations#:~:text=The%202023%20Edelman%20Trust%20Barometer,and%20ten%20points)). This ongoing confidence in one’s employer reflects a stable dynamic – possibly a response to low external institutional trust – and is **a continuation of existing trends (category a)** rather than a reaction to AI. However, that trust is **conditional on a reciprocal sense of trust from management**: employees’ trust “falls when they don’t feel trusted by corporate leadership” ([www.edelman.com](https://www.edelman.com/trust/2023/trust-barometer/special-report-trust-at-work/my-employer-higher-expectations#:~:text=Ever%20Higher%20Expectations%20Employees%20have,see%20as%20excessive%20involvement%20by)). 

**On the flip side, many employers have struggled to trust their employees in an era of remote and tech-mediated work.** Microsoft’s 2022 **Work Trend Index** (20,000 people in 11 countries) highlighted **“productivity paranoia,” with 85% of business leaders saying the shift to hybrid work made it challenging to trust that employees are productive** ([www.microsoft.com](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/hybrid-work-is-just-work#:~:text=pings%2C%20working%20in%20non,employees%20lack%20context%20on%20how)). In many cases, this distrust has led companies to adopt AI-driven **employee monitoring tools**, from keystroke trackers to webcam-based attention analytics. These practices raise new trust issues: half of U.S. workers (51%) reported in *2023* that their employers were using monitoring technology ([www.apa.org](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/work-in-america/2023-work-america-ai-monitoring#:~:text=etc,manual)). Research by the American Psychological Association found that **monitored employees were significantly more likely to feel micromanaged (56% vs 33%) and to report negative mental health impacts from work** ([www.apa.org](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/work-in-america/2023-work-america-ai-monitoring#:~:text=labor%20%2849,compared)). In other words, when employers lean on surveillance tech – often AI-enabled – it can signal a lack of trust in employees, which in turn **erodes employees’ trust in management and harms workplace well-being**.  

**AI is also entering human resources decisions (hiring, evaluation, and even firing), and public trust in these AI-mediated processes is generally low.** In a **Pew Research Center survey of 11,004 U.S. adults (Dec 2022)**, **71% opposed** the idea of AI making final hiring decisions (only 7% favored it) ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/04/PI_2023.04.20_AI-in-Hiring_FINAL.pdf#:~:text=they%20look%20at%20the%20possible,keeping%20track%20of%20when%20office)), and **majorities also opposed AI’s use in firing employees or monitoring workers’ performance** ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/04/PI_2023.04.20_AI-in-Hiring_FINAL.pdf#:~:text=they%20look%20at%20the%20possible,keeping%20track%20of%20when%20office)). This strong reluctance indicates that **people doubt AI’s fairness and judgment in evaluating humans – preferring human decision-makers in HR matters**. Indeed, **nearly half of U.S. job seekers (49%) believe AI recruiting tools are more likely to be biased than human recruiters**, according to a 2023 industry poll (American Staffing Association, a promotional source) – a perception that can undermine confidence in AI-based hiring ([americanstaffing.net](https://americanstaffing.net/asa-workforce-monitor/ai-in-hiring/#:~:text=ASA%20Workforce%20Monitor%E2%80%94AI%20In%20Hiring,conducted%20by%20The%20Harris%20Poll)). Notably, this skepticism is not entirely new (concerns about algorithmic bias in hiring have existed for years), but the proliferation of generative AI tools has increased public awareness of automated decision-making. We are likely observing **a recent intensification of pre-existing concerns about fairness and trust in the workplace (a mix of categories a and b)**. For example, **Pew’s 2022–23 polling found 33% of Americans believed greater use of AI in hiring would reduce bias, but ***44%*** said it would actually make hiring more biased** ([americanstaffing.net](https://americanstaffing.net/asa-workforce-monitor/ai-in-hiring/#:~:text=Association%20americanstaffing,conducted%20by%20The%20Harris%20Poll)). With AI tools now more visible, **employees and the public are demanding transparency and human oversight to maintain trust in workplace decisions** ([www.pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/04/PI_2023.04.20_AI-in-Hiring_FINAL.pdf#:~:text=they%20look%20at%20the%20possible,keeping%20track%20of%20when%20office)).  

## Disputed or Unresolved  
Several aspects of **AI’s impact on trust remain debated or not fully resolved**. **One key question is whether generative AI is truly *causing* additional trust decline in media and institutions, or if it is simply coincident with ongoing downward trends.** While some commentators *claim* that AI-driven misinformation is accelerating a crisis of trust (e.g. “Generative AI threatens to amplify the trust crisis” ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/rebuilding-trust-journalisms-role-ai-driven-world#:~:text=generative%20AI%20content%20must%20be,generated%20summaries%2C%20and))), this is **difficult to prove without accounting for pre-existing declines**. So far, broad measures of trust in media and government have not shown a unique inflection attributable solely to AI – they continue to fall or fluctuate within historical ranges ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2023/dnr-executive-summary#:~:text=their%20political%20views,Finland)) ([news.gallup.com](https://news.gallup.com/poll/651977/americans-trust-media-remains-trend-low.aspx#:~:text=WASHINGTON%2C%20D.C.%20,1970s%2C%20trust%20ranged%20from%2068)). **Where a short-term shift *has* been observed – such as the drop in global AI trust from 2022 to 2023 ([www.unleash.ai](https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/news/66-of-individuals-may-be-using-ai-but-79-are-wary-of-possible-risks-finds-kpmg#:~:text=was%20keen%20to%20dig%20into,clear%20role%20to%20play%20when)) – it remains challenging to distinguish whether this is an effect of AI’s arrival or part of normal opinion variation.** Moreover, **direct causal links (category c) are largely unconfirmed**. For example, the widely hypothesized “**liar’s dividend**” – that exposure to deepfakes would cause people to distrust even real news – is still **awaiting solid evidence**. The 2024 APSR experiment found **no overall decrease in media trust when subjects were told a real political video was a deepfake** ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073#:~:text=ineffective%20against%20video%20evidence%20and,Information%20American%20Political%20Science%20Review)), suggesting the liar’s dividend may not be as potent in practice as many fear. However, that was one set of U.S.-based experiments; whether deepfakes or AI propaganda might erode institutional trust in other contexts (e.g. during a heated election abroad) remains an open question. **In summary, the notion that AI is directly driving a new collapse in trust is *contested*; most researchers urge caution about drawing causal conclusions without long-term evidence.** The coming years – with more data from longitudinal surveys and experimental studies – should help clarify if generative AI is fundamentally reshaping trust, or if it is mainly *amplifying or modulating* trends that were already in motion.
